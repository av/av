You type six lazy words into a terminal, asking an agent to <span data-fx="type">add retries to the upload client</span>, and walk away to make coffee. Twenty minutes later you come back to a green branch that is <span data-fx="alarm">quietly trying to take down production</span>. The patch retries on HTTP 400 responses, so a single malformed payload will hammer your auth service until it falls over. A drive-by config change sits in the diff uninvited, and when one integration test failed, the agent <span data-fx="rewrite">rewrote the assertion</span> until the suite turned green.

You spend the next ten minutes cutting, rejecting the 400 retry, rewriting the backoff math by hand, reverting the config hunk, and killing a sibling run mid-turn before it can gut another test file. That <span data-fx="cut">ten-minute cut</span> is the only place your engineering brain touched the work.

What you asked the agent first was mostly noise that any developer on your team would have typed the same way. The real signal lives in what you <span data-fx="reject">reject</span>, <span data-fx="edit">edit</span>, <span data-fx="revert">revert</span>, or <span data-fx="stop">stop mid-run</span>, because that correction delta is the exact <span data-fx="gradient">gradient of your taste and judgment</span>. <span data-fx="del">Your prompts are worthless</span>, and <span data-fx="add">your diffs are your weights</span>.

Most engineers throw those weights in the trash ten times a day. You fix the mistake in your working tree, merge the pull request, and close the window, only for a fresh instance of the same model to wake up tomorrow morning with <span data-fx="forget">complete amnesia</span> and walk straight into the same trap. If you fix an agent's mistake three times without mining the trace into a rule, a test, or a script, you have inverted the relationship. You are working as an <span data-fx="loop">unpaid biological retry loop</span> for a stateless model that forgets you the moment the thread closes.

The model only forgets you on your own machine, while upstream <span data-fx="rec">the vendor keeps the tape</span>. Cursor's Tab model [trains through online reinforcement learning on whether developers accept or reject its suggestions](https://cursor.com/blog/tab-rl) across more than <span data-fx="count" data-to="400">400</span> million requests a day, assigning <span data-fx="pos">+0.75</span> to an accepted completion and <span data-fx="neg">-0.25</span> to a rejected one in their published reward example. Training on that stream cut the suggestions the model showed by <span data-fx="bar" data-from="100" data-to="79">21%</span> and lifted its accept rate by <span data-fx="bar" data-from="100" data-to="128">28%</span>. Every time an engineer hit <span data-fx="key">Escape</span> on a bad completion, they computed <span data-fx="gift">a free gradient step for Cursor's weights</span>, trading their rejection signal for a slightly quieter autocomplete the next morning.

Tool vendors were the first to harvest that correction stream, and employers are close behind. In April 2026, Meta [told employees it would <span data-fx="cursor">record keystrokes, button clicks, and mouse movements</span>](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/21/meta-will-record-employees-keystrokes-and-use-it-to-train-its-ai-models/) in selected work applications to train agents for everyday computer tasks, explaining that "our models need real examples of how people actually use them." For software engineers, a company does not even need a screen recorder because coding harnesses already turn human judgment into structured telemetry. Claude Code [logs a <span data-fx="decision"><code>tool_decision</code></span> event every time a user accepts or rejects a tool call](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage), and an administrator can switch that OpenTelemetry stream on for every seat through managed settings and <span data-fx="lock">lock the collector endpoint</span> it reports to.

Follow that pipe forward a couple of years and the forty-year bargain of software employment breaks. The old deal was that the company kept the code you typed on the clock, while you walked out the door with the experience inside your skull. Centralize the agent trace logs across five hundred engineers and the company captures <span data-fx="clone">a working copy of the experience</span> too. Every time a staff engineer aborts a broken migration, blocks a leaky fixture, or rewrites a naive lock, they label a training example of institutional taste. An employer that logs those trajectories can hill-climb its internal workflows on its staff's judgment until <span data-fx="swap">each role is replaceable</span>. Without your own local trace intelligence, your employer compounds on your judgment while you walk away from the job with <span data-fx="drain">zero weights</span>, making asymmetry of trace capture the new wealth <span data-fx="gap">gap</span> at work.

Staying on the right side of that gap requires keeping your own ledger in a form you can carry out the building. <span data-fx="del">Your resume is dead paper</span> once anyone with a credit card can spin up a fleet of coding agents before lunch, whereas <span data-fx="add">your eval suite is portable capital</span>. Hiring a senior principal will come to mean hiring their <span data-fx="harness">personal harness</span>, built from the curated failure traces, hard gates, and decision rubrics they accumulated across years of steering agents. Technical interviews will eventually stop asking candidates to invert trees on a whiteboard and start mounting the candidate's harness onto a messy repository to see <span data-fx="block">what it blocks before merge</span>.

Building that personal trace loop is easier than it sounds, and it will <span data-fx="bruise">bruise your ego</span>. Before you inspect your own logs, it is flattering to believe that senior intuition is a deep, mysterious craft built over a decade. Cluster your own intervention traces across a thousand runs and you discover that <span data-fx="fill" data-to="90">roughly ninety percent</span> of what you call your judgment is <span data-fx="dots">fifteen repeatable heuristics</span> firing on a loop. I steer agents across four machines over Tailscale most days, and once raw JSONL transcripts piled up past what I could grep by hand, I started using <span data-fx="replay"><code>tracequest</code></span> to index, search, and replay session traces across harnesses so I could see why I kept stepping in.

Almost none of my repeated corrections turned out to be profound architectural insights. Today they live as <span data-fx="add">files and checks on my machines</span> instead of <span data-fx="del">thoughts in my head</span>. On my Linux box `fedora`, agents kept editing `~/.hermes/config.yaml` and wondering why nothing changed, until a note in my home directory warned them that the Docker container mounts `~/.hermes/data/config.yaml` as `/opt/data/config.yaml` instead. Across my repos, I wasted hours rescuing agents that blamed application logic when a test failed, until I compiled a rule to treat friction as environmental until proven otherwise, checking the working directory, dependencies, and permissions before touching code. Parallel agents kept forking database migrations until a rule forced an Alembic head linearity check before any merge. Other rules ban polling CI in sleep loops in favor of one blocking `gh pr checks --watch` call, and require reading the full diff against the base branch to revert stray hunks before reporting a task done. Each of those fifteen or so vetoes can be <span data-fx="compile">compiled out of your head</span> in an afternoon, and what remains once they are gone is <span data-fx="rare">the small fraction of your judgment that is rare</span>.

Once that trace pipeline is running, you realize the instrument is <span data-fx="mirror">pointed at both ends of the terminal</span>. We spend plenty of energy tracking whether frontier models degrade on Tuesday afternoons while ignoring the much noisier biological system at the keyboard. <span data-fx="drift">Cognitive drift</span> shows up in your traces days before you feel it in your body. When you are tired at midnight or stretched across ten concurrent worktrees, your acceptance threshold <span data-fx="drop">drops</span>, your prompts get <span data-fx="blur">vague</span>, and you wave through a three-hundred-line diff because the tests are green and you want to go to sleep. Fatigue numbs the exact faculty you use to judge your own sharpness, so you rarely feel the slide until <span data-fx="spike">your revert rate spikes two days later</span>. Your personal trace log is <span data-fx="mirror">a mirror of your own blind spots</span>, catching you degrading the same way a canary catches a regressing model.

Closing that loop every week also moves the line between you and the machine. Each time your personal trace review turns a manual review into an automated check, the surface area where a human is needed shrinks, forcing you up the <span data-fx="ladder">entropy ladder</span> toward the decisions you still cannot formalize. You spend less time policing lockfiles or retry codes and more time deciding which project deserves doing at all, when a technically clean patch is wrong for the business, or which operational risk is worth taking this quarter. <span data-fx="boundary">The boundary of "you" moves every week</span>, and it only moves upward if you keep <span data-fx="compile">compiling the layer below it</span>.

That makes a personal trace archive the most sensitive record you generate, and it rewrites what privacy needs to protect. A leaked inbox tells someone what you worked on last month, while a leaked trace history gives them <span data-fx="clone">a training set to clone what you would approve, ignore, or veto next</span>. Privacy in the agent era means <span data-fx="boundary">guarding your decision boundary</span> rather than <span data-fx="del">hiding your messages</span>, yet most engineers stream that boundary to tool vendors and employers in exchange for a faster autocomplete. Keep your traces on your own machines, mine them into your own harness, and guard the line where you say <span data-fx="stamp">no</span>.
